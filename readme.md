II. Abstract This paper proposes a new algorithm - the Intrinsic Time-Scale Decomposition (ITD), which enables efficient and accurate time-frequency energy (TFE) analysis of signals. This method overcomes the limitations of classical methods (such as those based on Fourier transform or wavelet transform) and more recent methods (such as those based on empirical mode decomposition) in analyzing nonlinear and/or non-stationary signals. The ITD method decomposes the signal into two parts: (1) a series of intrinsic rotational components (with clearly defined instantaneous frequency and amplitude); (2) a monotonic trend. This decomposition can precisely preserve the critical points and the temporal information of overlapping waves of the signal, with time resolution consistent with the scale at which extreme values appear in the input signal. Moreover, this paper demonstrates how the ITD supports single wave analysis and thereby fosters a powerful new class of real-time signal filters that extract and utilize the inherent instantaneous amplitude, frequency/phase information of the signal, while also incorporating other relevant morphological features.  
III. Introduction Signal and other data analysis are an essential part of pure theoretical research and applied research across all disciplines. Real-world data often exhibit non-stationarity, noise, and originates from potential nonlinear systems or processes, and we often know little or nothing about their dynamics. However, most tools currently used for quantitative analysis of such signals are better suited for linear systems, low-noise or noisy stationary signals.  
For any complex signal source, the development of time-frequency energy (TFE) analysis methods has always been centered around the following objectives:  
(1) Achieving a delicate balance between precise time information and precise frequency information, to obtain timescales determined by the frequencies contained within the signal itself;
(2) Avoid making a priori assumptions about the content/form of the analyzed signal (e.g., by decomposing "unstructured function dependencies");
(3) Perform analysis in an efficient and fast manner (ideally online real-time analysis).
For a long time, Fourier transform has been the mainstream tool for TFE analysis of various signals and data, despite its inherent assumption that signals are stationary. To address this limitation in non-stationary signal analysis, researchers typically employ Fourier transforms on demeaned (mean-subtracted) and/or tapered (steeply sloped) data within moving windows or use linear, finite impulse response (FIR) or infinite impulse response (IIR) filters to extract the required TFE information. However, it is well known that these methods face a difficult choice: either obtain good TFE information frequency resolution with long windows/filters at the expense of time resolution, or obtain good time resolution with short windows/filters at the expense of frequency resolution. Moreover, although Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is efficient, it uses a rectangular time-frequency plane to acquire information, resulting in blurred time information of high-frequency fluctuations throughout the window. Analysis based on Fourier transform also has other drawbacks, such as introducing spurious harmonics and requiring a wide spectrum to represent transient changes (such as pulses or step signals) or other time-non-stationary data.
Over the past few decades, methods for obtaining TFE information based on wavelet transform have gradually become popular, thanks to the bifurcation of the time-frequency plane by the transform—it better localizes time to high-frequency signal variations and provides superior frequency information for low-frequency variations. The main advantage of wavelet analysis over Fourier analysis lies in its ability to provide TFE information more closely matched to the temporal scale of signal variation through scaling and shifting of basis functions with compact support.
The Fourier analysis, wavelet analysis, and most other TFE (Time-Frequency Analysis) methods (such as those based on the Wiener-Ville distribution or evolutionary spectral methods) all have an inherent flaw: they represent signals using basis functions predefined a priori, and the performance of these methods is closely related to the degree of representation of the signal's morphology by the chosen basis functions. For example, sound waves produced by orchestras consist of superimposed pure tones with characteristic harmonics and fixed durations, which can be well characterized by Fourier analysis; however, more complex non-stationary signals (such as indoor noise from an environment, meteorological signals, financial signals, or electroencephalograms) are difficult (if not impossible) to construct suitable basis functions in advance, nor can accurate time segmentation be performed with predefined windows because the precise timing and time scale of signal changes are often unknown.
Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) is another TFE analysis method dedicated to solving these challenges, capable of decomposing physical signals (including nonlinear or non-stationary signals) into a series of Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs)—the proponents of EMD claim that these IMFs represent the inherent oscillation modes in the physical signal. However, EMD has several practical limitations that reduce its applicability and may lead to inaccurate descriptions of signal dynamics, such as difficulty in continuously generating intrinsic rotation components, the need for iterative filtering processes, boundary effects, and issues associated with spline interpolation.
This paper proposes a new TFE analysis method—Intrinsic Time Scale Decomposition (ITD), specifically designed for nonlinear or non-stationary signals generated by complex systems with multiple time scale dynamics from any source. It overcomes the limitations of classical methods such as EMD and Fourier transforms, wavelets, etc.
